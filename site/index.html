A real case: the request was in the last paragraph.

Document 08 from the benchmark, and what one model produced from it with Minto installed. Both texts are quoted in full, exactly as they were run and blind-judged.

What we call bad

The source message

Hi Alex,

I am increasingly worried about the defect backlog. The attached chart compares new defects with the number we close and projects the backlog one year ahead.

Customer Experience and Research also report that defects are contributing to churn. A second chart places churn beside defect volume, and an interview illustrates the same issue.

There are signs of brand damage too: user posts, a complaint from a major account, and Sales reports that these concerns are making deals harder to close.

I recommend using the next sprint to reduce the defect backlog. Let me know what you think.

Why it is badAlex has to read four paragraphs of evidence before finding out what he is being asked to decide.
What we call good

Opus 5, with Minto

Blind judge: structure 8/8, quality 10/10

Hi Alex,

Let’s spend the next sprint on the defect backlog — we’re losing customers and deals to it faster than we’re closing it.

  • The backlog will keep growing on its own. We’re opening defects faster than we close them; the attached chart projects where that leaves us in a year if we don’t intervene now.
  • Defects are costing us customers. Customer Experience and Research tie them directly to churn — the second chart puts churn alongside defect volume, and the attached interview shows the mechanism up close.
  • They’re now costing us new business too. User posts and a complaint from one of our largest accounts have made this visible outside the product, and Sales reports it’s slowing close rates.

Can you confirm the sprint focus so we can shape the backlog before planning?

Shown in full. The judge flagged one hard failure — an invented fact — in this output and in the control output alike.

Why it is goodThe ask is the first sentence. Three points of one kind — all of them things the reader loses by waiting — and the buried recommendation moved to the top.

Being honest about this one: it is an easy case, and the skill did not win it. The recommendation was already written, only in the wrong place. Opus’s own control run also scored 8/8 and 10/10 here, and the judge flagged an invented fact in both. What separates the two arms is the pattern across all 172 runs below, not a knockout on any single document. Read the blind verdict ↗

Benchmark, 172 judged runs

Five measures improve. One gets worse.

Eleven documents, eight models, each rewritten twice: once with Minto and once with a prompt that only names the Pyramid Principle. A blind judge scored all 172 without knowing which was which. Here is every number it produced.

In raw points, pooled across all eight models: structure 4.76 → 5.27 out of 8, quality 7.17 → 7.90 out of 10. Applying the rubric’s own penalty for invented or dropped facts gives 6.34 → 7.08.

One run per model, arm and document, so repeatability is unmeasured. The control is not an unprompted model: it is a one-sentence prompt that already names the Pyramid Principle, which is why it scores as well as it does. Two results move against the skill and are published all the same: models given it asserted unsupported facts more often — 49 runs against 42 — and put the answer in the opening sentence less often, which are the top fixes for the next version. Quality improved for all eight models; structure fell for one, Sonnet 5, and stayed level for qwen3.8-27b. Three of the eleven documents are new in this round, so these figures are not comparable with the previous 112-run table. Every figure here is computed from the published verdicts by a script, not summarised by a model.
