What the judge saw across all outputs

The stronger outputs put the sprint recommendation first, expose three business-loss reasons, and keep each evidence unit beneath its proper reason. Weaker outputs either omit or conflate evidence, attach operational data to the wrong business-loss branch, merge distinct losses into a broad paragraph, or mix supporting reasons with follow-on actions.

What the judge asked of every output

  1. Is the recommendation first, middle, or last?
  2. Is the first-level kind business losses, mixed, or actions/data?
  3. How many first-level elements appear?
  4. Are all nine evidence units placed beneath the right reason?
  5. Was backlog growth used as SCQ Complication rather than a reason, and if so was that stronger?
  6. Is the format a colleague message or an oversized formal memo?
  7. Which numbers, customers, dates, or facts were invented?
  8. Is the reader's question stated literally?

Gold 08: technical-debt message

This is an original modern fixture inspired by the cited Planio example, not a
book-derived gold text.

Expected structure

Top: Use the next sprint to reduce the defect backlog.

First-level reasons:

  1. The backlog is growing faster than the team can reduce it.
  2. Defects are driving customer churn.
  3. Defects are damaging brand perception and sales.

Evidence stays beneath its reason: incoming/fixed/projection data under backlog
growth; CX, interview, and churn chart under churn; user posts, account
complaint, and Sales reports under brand and sales.

The recommendation must move from the last paragraph to the first sentence.
Specific percentages, customer names, deadlines, or defect counts are
inventions. A compact colleague message is preferable to a formal memo.

An output that treats backlog growth as the SCQ Complication and uses only the
two external business losses as its key line may be stronger than this gold and
must not be penalized.
