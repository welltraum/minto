Audit: "Period Graph Books"

1. No governing thought

The memo never states an answer. It ends with "Could we discuss how best to proceed?" — a request for a meeting, not a recommendation. Management asked for corrective proposals; the document delivers a description of a process plus a diagnosis, and leaves the reader to infer what should be done.

The nearest thing to a point ("The production process badly needs streamlining") appears in the second-to-last paragraph, buried inside the "Overall assessment" section, and is asserted rather than supported as the top of a pyramid. The real message — visible only by reading between the lines — appears to be roughly: consolidate end-to-end responsibility for graph-book production in one place, and give the team the resources to redesign the process while continuing to produce the books. That message must be stated first, at the top.

2. Structure is chronological-descriptive, not deductive or inductive

The sequence is: history of the transfer → how production works → what goes wrong → summary judgment → request to talk. This is the order in which the author investigated the problem, not the order in which the reader needs to receive it. The pyramid is inverted: findings support a conclusion that arrives last.

The "Production" section is a four-step process narrative with no argumentative role. As written it is background the reader must hold in memory for several paragraphs before learning why it matters. Under the principle, process detail belongs below the line as support for a specific assertion (e.g. "the process has four hand-offs and three separate data entries"), or in an appendix — not as a free-standing section.

3. The introduction does not establish Situation–Complication–Question

There is no S-C-Q. The opening paragraph gives Situation (transfer of responsibility since Period 5) and then announces the document's own contents ("This memo describes issues that appeared after the transfer"), which is a table of contents, not a Complication. The Complication that actually motivates the document — errors and inconsistencies are appearing in books shown to senior management — is never stated as such, and the Question it raises ("what should we do about it?") is therefore never posed. Because no question is posed, nothing in the body can be shown to answer one.

The reason management commissioned the work — their own request for corrective proposals — is also absent, so the document does not visibly respond to its brief.

4. Grouping defects in the body
  • "Issues" mixes categories. The section runs together causes (fragmentation, an extra person inserted, three departments, triple data entry) and symptoms (inconsistent data between periods and regions, incorrect variable-cost calculations, unexplained point changes, re-entry of prior-period data). These belong at different levels: symptoms are evidence; fragmentation is the cause they support. Presented side by side, neither supports the other and the logical relationship is left to the reader.
  • The single-book example is unlabeled. The second paragraph of "Issues" narrates one book's data path, then lists problems. It is unclear whether this is an illustration of a general pattern or a special case confined to that book. Its role in the argument is undefined.
  • The final list is not MECE. "Unexplained changes to points that were previously correct" and "inconsistent data between periods and regions" overlap; "prior-period data that must be re-entered" is a process defect, not a data error, and sits in a list of data errors.
  • "Overall assessment" carries three unrelated ideas in one paragraph: (a) the cause is process fragmentation, (b) no one owns the data end to end, (c) the team lacks capacity to redesign while producing. (c) is a resourcing conclusion, not part of the diagnosis, and it is the closest thing in the memo to a recommendation — yet it is presented as an aside in a summary.
5. Headings do not carry meaning

"Production," "Issues," "Overall assessment" are topic labels. They tell the reader what subject follows but assert nothing, so skimming the headings yields no argument. Each heading should state the point made beneath it, so that the headings alone reproduce the pyramid's second level.

6. Unsupported assertions

Several key claims are stated without the evidence a decision-maker would need:

  • "The same information may be entered into computer systems three times" — "may be" hedges the central fact of the case. Either it is three times or it is not.
  • "The current team cannot manage the required changes as well as ongoing production" — no volume, headcount, or time figures are offered, though this claim carries the resource request.
  • "Most problems arise from a cumbersome fragmented process" — asserted, not demonstrated; no problem is traced to a specific hand-off.
  • No quantification anywhere: how many errors, in which books, over how many periods, with what consequence in front of senior management.
7. The ending abdicates

The closing question transfers the analytical burden back to the reader. Financial Analysis owns the process and the diagnosis; it is the party positioned to propose the fix. A document that diagnoses without recommending forces the reader to do the author's work, and it gives management nothing to approve or reject.

What must change
  1. Lead with the recommendation. Open with the action proposed — consolidated ownership of end-to-end production, plus whatever resourcing that requires — before any supporting detail.
  2. Build a proper introduction. Situation: Financial Analysis assumed production of the four books in Period 5. Complication: errors and inconsistencies are reaching senior management. Question: what should be done? The answer to that question is the governing thought.
  3. Separate cause from symptom into distinct levels. Symptoms as evidence beneath a single assertion about data reliability; fragmentation and unclear ownership as the cause; capacity as a separate, third supporting point about implementation.
  4. Demote the process description. Keep only the facts that prove the fragmentation point (hand-off count, duplicate entries, number of parties); move the rest to an appendix.
  5. Make the supporting groups MECE and rewrite the error list so that data defects and process defects are not intermingled.
  6. Convert every heading into an assertion, so the headings alone read as the argument.
  7. Quantify the three load-bearing claims: frequency and impact of errors, number of duplicate entries, and the capacity gap justifying additional resources.
  8. Replace the closing question with a specific ask — the decision required, from whom, and by when.

# Fixture 03: Period Graph Books

**Mode:** `audit`
**Language:** `en`

## Context

Corporate Financial Analysis recently took over production of four monthly
graph books used in presentations to senior management. Management knows that
errors and inconsistencies have appeared and asked for corrective proposals.

## Before

```text
Subject: Period Graph Books

Since Period 5, Financial Analysis has been responsible for producing four
graph-presentation books previously handled by Corporate Planning. This memo
describes issues that appeared after the transfer.

Production

1. Gather data from divisional forms, internal documents, and telephone calls.
2. Calculate graph points manually or on the corporate computer, including
   rolling values for revenue, cost, and percentages.
3. Transcribe the points to input sheets. Analysts update the current period,
   then Color Graphics re-enters the points into its own database and generates
   the graphs.
4. Check the data and calculations.

Issues

The transfer inserted another person into an already fragmented process.
Divisional staff and three corporate departments participate, and the same
information may be entered into computer systems three times.

For one book, divisional data are loaded into a dedicated corporate program,
calculated, transferred to Color Graphics input sheets, entered again, and
returned for updates. Problems include inconsistent data between periods and
regions, incorrect variable-cost calculations, unexplained changes to points
that were previously correct, and prior-period data that must be re-entered.

Overall assessment

Most problems arise from a cumbersome fragmented process. No one controls the
data from beginning to end, responsibility is unclear, and errors can fall
between stages. The production process badly needs streamlining, both for each
book and for common calculations. The current team cannot manage the required
changes as well as ongoing production.

Could we discuss how best to proceed?
```

Top: Remove the opportunities for error across data transfer, graph-point
