# Minto Audit — "Period Graph Books"

## Verdict

The memo fails at the pyramid's most important level: it has no governing thought. It is a chronological description of a process and a list of symptoms, ending in a request to talk. The reader — senior management, who already knows errors exist and asked for corrective proposals — reaches the last line without a recommendation, an owner, or a cost. The document answers "what is wrong?" when the question on the table is "what should we do?"

## Defects

**1. No answer at the top (missing governing thought).**
The opening paragraph is background: who handed off what, in which period. The memo's actual message sits buried in the final section — "the production process badly needs streamlining" plus the concession that "the current team cannot manage the required changes as well as ongoing production." That is the answer, and it belongs in the first three lines. As written, the reader must construct the point themselves from evidence delivered before its purpose is known.

**2. Introduction does not establish Situation–Complication–Question.**
The Situation (Financial Analysis assumed production in Period 5) is present. The Complication (errors and inconsistencies now appear in books used with senior management) is never stated — it is only implied, and only by the memo's existence. The Question the reader is asking ("how do we fix this?") is therefore never raised, so nothing that follows is visibly an answer to anything. The closing "Could we discuss how best to proceed?" is the Question migrating to the end of the document, which is a reliable sign the introduction did no work.

**3. Wrong structural ordering: time order used where deductive or problem-solution order is required.**
"Production" is a four-step chronological narrative. Process steps are the *support* for a claim about fragmentation, not the message itself. Presented first and unlabelled, they read as documentation of a workflow rather than evidence that the workflow is broken. The reader has no criterion for what matters in the list until three sections later.

**4. The key line does not group; it accumulates.**
The problems appear as a run-on inventory: inconsistent data between periods and regions, incorrect variable-cost calculations, unexplained changes to previously correct points, prior-period data requiring re-entry. These are neither mutually exclusive nor collectively organized. Some are *causes* (redundant re-entry), some are *effects* (inconsistency across periods), and some are *unexplained observations* (points changing for no known reason). Mixing cause, effect, and mystery at one level of abstraction means the group summarizes to nothing — it fails the test that every grouping must yield an inference beyond the mere sum of its items.

**5. Levels of abstraction are collapsed.**
The one-book example — data loaded into a dedicated program, calculated, transferred to input sheets, re-entered, returned for updates — sits at the same visual and grammatical rank as the general finding that three corporate departments participate and information is entered three times. The specific instance should subordinate to the general claim, illustrating it. Instead it competes with it.

**6. Assertions are unquantified, so no decision can be taken from them.**
"Most problems," "badly needs streamlining," "cannot manage the required changes" — each is a judgment offered without magnitude. How many errors, in which books, at what cost in analyst hours or in credibility with the audience? Absent numbers, management cannot size a remedy, and the memo cannot support the resourcing request it is plainly angling toward.

**7. The real ask is unstated.**
The final two sentences together amount to a request for additional headcount or for relief from production duties during a redesign. This is the memo's operative point and it is delivered obliquely, as an aside, phrased in the negative. A pyramid document names its recommendation.

**8. Section labels carry no message.**
"Production," "Issues," "Overall assessment" describe categories of content, not conclusions. Minto headings should be able to be read in sequence and convey the argument. These could head any memo about any process.

## What must change

Restructure so the governing thought — *the fragmented, multi-hand process should be consolidated under single ownership, and the team needs X to do it while sustaining production* — stands at the apex, stated in the first paragraph.

Rebuild the introduction as Situation (production transferred in Period 5), Complication (books presented to senior management now contain errors traceable to the handoff), Question (how should production be fixed?), leading directly to the answer.

Replace the accumulated symptom list with a genuine key line of two or three mutually exclusive supporting points that each answer "why?" of the governing thought — plausibly: no single owner controls the data end to end; the same data is keyed three times across three departments; verification occurs after transcription rather than at the source. Demote the current symptoms to evidence beneath whichever point they support, and separate causes from effects rather than interleaving them.

Subordinate the process description and the one-book example to the points they evidence; neither is a top-level section.

Quantify the error rate, the duplicated effort, and the resourcing gap, then convert the closing question into a named recommendation with an owner and a decision the reader is being asked to make.
