**Audit of the “Period Graph Books” memo against the Minto Pyramid Principle**

| Minto Principle Element | Defect(s) Identified | Why it matters (impact on clarity / persuasiveness) | What must change |
|--------------------------|----------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------|
| **Overall Structure (Top‑Down hierarchy)** | • The memo opens with a narrative description rather than a clear **key line** (the single answer to the central question). <br>• No explicit “Situation‑Complication‑Question‑Answer” (SCQA) framing. | Readers cannot instantly see the main recommendation or decision needed; they must wade through background before grasping the purpose. | • Insert a concise opening paragraph that states the **key line** (e.g., “We recommend consolidating the graph‑book production process into a single, controlled workflow to eliminate duplication and errors.”). <br>• Follow with a brief SCQA lead‑in that sets the situation, identifies the complication, poses the decision question, and previews the answer. |
| **Logical Grouping (MECE)** | • “Production” and “Issues” sections contain overlapping items (e.g., data entry steps appear in both). <br>• The list of production steps mixes **activities** (gather data) with **methods** (manual calculation) and **responsibilities** (analysts, Color Graphics) without clear grouping. <br>• “Overall assessment” repeats points already made in “Issues” without new synthesis. | Redundant or overlapping points dilute the argument and make it harder to see distinct problem categories. | • Re‑organize into **MECE** buckets: <br>  1. **Current Process Flow** (chronological steps, single list). <br>  2. **Pain Points** (grouped by cause: duplication, lack of ownership, calculation errors). <br>  3. **Impact** (inconsistent data, re‑work, risk). <br>  4. **Recommendation** (single, actionable proposal). |
| **Headings & Signposting** | • Headings are generic (“Production”, “Issues”, “Overall assessment”) and do not convey the logical purpose of each section. <br>• No sub‑headings to guide the reader through the argument. | Readers cannot scan for the information they need; the memo feels like a flat narrative. | • Use **action‑oriented headings** that reflect the pyramid logic, e.g., “Current Process (What We Do Today)”, “Key Problems (Why It Fails)”, “Consequences (What It Costs Us)”, “Proposed Solution (How We Fix It)”. |
| **Key Line Placement** | • The memo ends with an open‑ended question (“Could we discuss how best to proceed?”) rather than a definitive recommendation. | The decision‑maker is left without a clear answer to act on; the memo’s purpose is ambiguous. | • Move the recommendation to the top (after the SCQA lead) and close with a specific next‑step (e.g., “We propose forming a cross‑functional task force to redesign the workflow within 30 days.”). |
| **Supporting Data & Evidence** | • Assertions about “inconsistent data”, “incorrect variable‑cost calculations”, etc., are not backed by concrete examples, frequencies, or impact metrics. | Lack of evidence weakens credibility and makes it difficult to prioritize actions. | • Within the “Pain Points” section, add **brief, quantifiable evidence** (e.g., “3 % of entries required re‑work each month, costing an average of 4 hours per analyst”). Keep it concise; the data should support each bullet, not become a separate narrative. |
| **Parallelism & Sentence Structure** | • Mixed verb forms (“Gather”, “Calculate”, “Transcribe”, “Check”) vs. later narrative sentences (“Problems include…”) break parallelism. <br>• Some sentences are long and contain multiple ideas (e.g., the third production step). | Inconsistent style hampers readability and the perception of a disciplined argument. | • Ensure **parallel construction** within each list (all verbs in same tense, same grammatical form). <br>• Split complex sentences into single‑idea statements. |
| **Clarity of Responsibility** | • The memo mentions “analysts”, “Color Graphics”, “divisional staff”, but does not map who owns each step or who is accountable for errors. | One of the core diagnoses (“no one controls the data”) is not explicitly linked to the current responsibility matrix, leaving the problem vague. | • Add a **responsibility matrix** (or a concise statement) that shows who does what, highlighting gaps. This can be a sub‑bullet under “Current Process”. |
| **Length & Conciseness** | • The memo includes narrative filler (“Since Period 5…”, “Could we discuss…”) that does not advance the argument. | Excess length reduces impact and may cause the reader to miss the key recommendation. | • Trim extraneous background; keep only what is needed to set the situation and support the recommendation. |
| **Call‑to‑Action (CTA)** | • The final CTA is vague (“Could we discuss…”) and does not specify who should act, when, or what the next step is. | Decision‑makers need a concrete next step to move forward. | • End with a **specific CTA** (e.g., “Approve the formation of a redesign team by 15 May; the team will deliver a streamlined workflow plan within 30 days.”). |

### Summary of Required Changes (without rewriting)

1. **Insert a clear key line and SCQA lead‑in at the top.**  
2. **Re‑structure the body into MECE sections** (Current Process → Pain Points → Impact → Recommendation).  
3. **Rename headings** to reflect logical purpose and add sub‑headings where needed.  
4. **Place the recommendation early** and replace the open‑ended question with a concrete CTA.  
5. **Provide brief, quantifiable evidence** for each problem statement.  
6. **Standardize parallelism** in lists and simplify complex sentences.  
7. **Clarify responsibility** for each step (who does what, who owns the data).  
8. **Trim unnecessary narrative** to keep the memo concise and focused.  
9. **Conclude with a specific, actionable next step** (who, what, when).  

Implementing these changes will align the memo with the Minto Pyramid Principle, delivering a top‑down, logically grouped, and decision‑oriented document that senior management can read quickly and act upon.
