## Minto Pyramid Audit

**Overall judgment:** The document identifies real operational problems, but it does not form a clear governing thought or answer management’s request for corrective proposals.

### 1. Missing top-level answer

The memo ends with “Could we discuss how best to proceed?” rather than stating a recommendation. Its governing thought should answer:

- What should management approve?
- Why is that action necessary now?
- What outcome will it produce?

“Streamlining” is too broad to function as a decision-ready recommendation.

### 2. The introduction does not form an SCQ

The opening gives background and says issues appeared, but it does not establish a compelling complication or frame the decision question.

It needs a concise sequence:

- **Situation:** Financial Analysis assumed responsibility for four monthly graph books.
- **Complication:** The inherited, fragmented process is producing inconsistent and incorrect outputs while increasing rework.
- **Question:** What changes are required to make production accurate, controlled, and sustainable?
- **Answer:** A specific corrective proposal.

### 3. Facts, process description, diagnosis, and recommendation are mixed

“Production,” “Issues,” and “Overall assessment” combine several levels of logic:

- process steps;
- examples of defects;
- causes of defects;
- organizational observations;
- a resource/capacity conclusion.

These should be separated so that each section supports one point at a time.

### 4. Supporting points are not mutually exclusive or collectively complete

The document’s concerns overlap:

- repeated entry,
- fragmented responsibility,
- inconsistent data,
- faulty calculations,
- unexplained changes,
- prior-period re-entry,
- insufficient capacity.

They are presented as a list rather than grouped into a small number of distinct, parallel arguments. The supporting structure should likely distinguish:

1. Data ownership and control are unclear.
2. The production workflow creates avoidable re-entry and reconciliation risk.
3. Calculation and change-control practices do not ensure accuracy.
4. The current team cannot both redesign the process and maintain monthly delivery.

### 5. Causal logic is asserted, not demonstrated

The memo says “most problems arise from” fragmentation, but it does not clearly connect each observed error to a specific failure point in the process. It should show:

- where data originate;
- where they are transformed or re-entered;
- who owns validation at each point;
- how each handoff creates the cited errors.

Without this, the diagnosis remains plausible but insufficiently proved.

### 6. Evidence is uneven and unprioritized

One book receives a detailed example, while the other three graph books are not characterized. The reader cannot tell:

- whether the issues are universal or isolated;
- which defects are most material;
- which books or stages should be addressed first;
- the operational impact of errors and rework.

The audit should require evidence organized by significance, frequency, and common root cause.

### 7. The conclusion introduces an unsupported resource request

“The current team cannot manage the required changes” is consequential, but the memo does not establish:

- what changes are required;
- the work involved;
- what capacity is missing;
- whether the needed response is staffing, authority, systems support, or temporary project support.

This conclusion needs its own supporting evidence.

### 8. The document does not convert diagnosis into corrective proposals

Management requested corrective proposals, yet no concrete proposal is presented. The revised logic must specify the decisions required, such as:

- establish end-to-end ownership;
- create a controlled master dataset and single calculation source;
- eliminate redundant re-entry where feasible;
- define validation, version, and change-control responsibilities;
- assign implementation capacity and timing.

### 9. The current order follows discovery rather than executive reasoning

The memo begins with detailed production mechanics, then lists issues, then offers a general assessment. Senior management should instead receive the conclusion first, followed by the few reasons that justify it, with process detail and examples only as evidence.
