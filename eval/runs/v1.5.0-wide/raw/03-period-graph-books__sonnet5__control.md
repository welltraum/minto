# Audit: Period Graph Books memo

## Governing Thought
No question/proposal is stated up front. The memo ends with "Could we discuss how best to proceed?"—a question the reader must answer, when Minto requires the writer's answer to sit at the top. The situation-complication-question-answer sequence is missing entirely; the reader gets narrative first, judgment last.

## Structure

**No pyramid — it's a chronology.** The memo is organized by *time and process step* (production stages 1–4, then issues, then assessment) rather than by the ideas needed to support a recommendation. This is a classic Minto trap: describing what happened instead of grouping findings by the logic they support.

**Key line is buried and under-supported.** "The production process badly needs streamlining... the current team cannot manage the required changes as well as ongoing production" is the real point (essentially: separate the fix-it work from the keep-it-running work). It appears as a throwaway closing assessment, not as a boxed conclusion with grouped, parallel support beneath it.

**"Issues" section mixes levels.** It lists a structural diagnosis (fragmentation, triple entry, unclear ownership) and then drops into a single book's specific defects (wrong variable-cost calcs, data drifting between periods/regions) at the same indentation level as the structural point. These are different levels of abstraction and need separate groupings — one is "why the process fails," the other is "evidence that it fails."

**Production steps 1–4 don't answer a question.** They describe *how the books are made*, not *why problems occur* or *what should change*. Under Minto, this section should be reframed as diagnostic support (e.g., "the process has three independent entry points" ) rather than a procedural walkthrough.

## What must change

1. **State the answer first.** Replace the closing question with a top-line recommendation (e.g., "We should separate process redesign from ongoing production, and propose to do X") so the reader gets the governing thought before the details.
2. **Regroup by argument, not by chronology.** Replace "Production / Issues / Overall assessment" with groupings that answer "why," each supporting the top recommendation — e.g., (a) ownership is fragmented across three departments, (b) data is re-entered multiple times, creating error points, (c) the current team lacks capacity to redesign while also running production.
3. **Separate structural diagnosis from illustrative evidence.** The specific book's defects (wrong calculations, inconsistent regional data) should become a supporting example under the fragmentation point, not a co-equal section.
4. **Make groupings parallel and exhaustive at each level.** Under "why streamlining is needed," each bullet should be logically parallel (a distinct cause) rather than mixing causes with symptoms with one book's specific problems.
5. **End with the action requested, not an open question.** If discussion is genuinely needed, frame it as "we recommend X; we'd like your agreement/input on Y," not a bare question that leaves the governing thought unstated.
