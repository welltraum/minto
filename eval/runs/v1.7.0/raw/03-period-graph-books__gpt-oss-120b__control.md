**Minto Pyramid Audit – “Period Graph Books” Memo**

| **Pyramid Element** | **Observed Defect** | **Why It Violates the Principle** | **What Must Change** |
|----------------------|---------------------|-----------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Overall Structure (Top‑Down)** | The memo opens with a descriptive title (“Subject: Period Graph Books”) and then drifts into a chronological narrative. There is no single, explicit **key message** (the “answer” that the reader should take away). | Minto requires the document to start with the *answer* (the main conclusion or recommendation) and then support it with grouped arguments. Without a top‑level statement the reader cannot quickly grasp the purpose. | Insert a concise **Executive Summary / Key Message** at the very beginning (e.g., “The current four‑book production process is fragmented, error‑prone, and unsustainable; we must redesign it into a single, controlled workflow and reassign ownership.”). |
| **SCQA (Situation‑Complication‑Question‑Answer)** | The memo presents a *situation* (transfer of responsibility) but the *complication* is scattered across “Production” and “Issues” sections without a clear transition. No explicit *question* is posed, and the *answer* is missing. | SCQA is the backbone of a Minto pyramid. Without a clear question (“How should we fix the production process?”) the reader cannot see the logical need for the recommendation. | Re‑frame the opening with a brief **Situation** (current state), a **Complication** (errors, duplication, lack of control), a **Question** (“What corrective actions should be taken?”) and then the **Answer** (the recommendation). |
| **Hierarchy & Grouping** | • “Production” and “Issues” are presented as separate numbered lists, but the items are not grouped into logical sub‑arguments (e.g., *Process Inefficiency*, *Data Integrity*, *Responsibility Gap*). <br>• The “Overall assessment” paragraph mixes diagnosis with a vague request for discussion. | Minto demands that supporting points be **MECE** (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) and organized under clear headings that roll up to the top‑level answer. Mixing process steps with problem symptoms blurs the hierarchy. | Re‑organize the body into **MECE** sections that each support the top‑level answer, e.g.: <br>1. **Process Inefficiency** – duplicated data entry, multiple systems. <br>2. **Data Integrity Risks** – inconsistent values, incorrect calculations. <br>3. **Governance Gap** – unclear ownership, no end‑to‑end control. <br>Each section should contain concise bullet points, not long narrative sentences. |
| **Logical Flow (Pyramid Logic)** | The memo jumps from a high‑level description of the transfer to a step‑by‑step production list, then to a list of issues, then to a vague “overall assessment.” The reader must work backward to see how the issues relate to the production steps. | In a pyramid, the **supporting arguments** should follow the same order as the **key message** and be presented in a logical sequence (e.g., *Why the process is flawed → What the consequences are → What must be done*). The current order is disjointed. | Re‑order the content to follow the logical chain: <br>1. **Why the current process fails** (duplicate steps, lack of control). <br>2. **Consequences** (errors, re‑work, delayed reporting). <br>3. **Proposed corrective actions** (streamline workflow, centralize data entry, assign clear ownership). |
| **Clarity & Brevity** | Sentences are long and contain multiple ideas (“Divisional staff and three corporate departments participate, and the same information may be entered into computer systems three times”). The memo also repeats the same point (duplication) in several places. | Minto stresses **concise, single‑idea statements** at each level. Redundancy dilutes the impact and makes the hierarchy harder to follow. | Rewrite each bullet/paragraph to convey **one idea only** and eliminate repetition. Use short, declarative statements. |
| **Actionability (Recommendation)** | The memo ends with “Could we discuss how best to proceed?” – a question rather than a concrete recommendation. | The pyramid’s bottom layer should be **actionable recommendations** that directly address the top‑level answer. A vague discussion request does not give the reader a clear next step. | Replace the discussion prompt with a **specific recommendation** (e.g., “We propose to consolidate the four books into a single automated reporting system, assign end‑to‑end ownership to the Financial Analysis team, and eliminate manual re‑entry of data.”). |
| **Use of Headings & Labels** | The memo uses “Production,” “Issues,” and “Overall assessment” as headings, but they are not aligned with the logical hierarchy (they are parallel rather than subordinate to the key message). | In a pyramid, headings should reflect the **levels of the argument** (Answer → Main Supporting Themes → Sub‑points). Current headings do not map to the top‑level answer. | Rename headings to reflect the supporting themes identified in the hierarchy (e.g., “Process Fragmentation,” “Data Quality Problems,” “Governance Deficit”). |
| **MECE Violations** | • “Divisional staff and three corporate departments participate” overlaps with “Color Graphics re‑enters the points” (both describe who does what). <br>• “Incorrect variable‑cost calculations” and “Unexplained changes to points” are both symptoms of the same root cause (lack of control). | Overlapping points break the **mutually exclusive** requirement, making the argument less crisp. | Consolidate overlapping items under a single sub‑point and ensure each supporting argument addresses a distinct cause or effect. |
| **Formatting Consistency** | Mixed use of numbered lists, bullet points, and free‑form paragraphs; inconsistent indentation; no visual separation between sections. | Consistent formatting helps the reader see the hierarchical levels at a glance. | Adopt a uniform style: <br>– Top‑level answer (bold, centered). <br>– Main headings (bold, left‑aligned). <br>– Sub‑points (bulleted, indented). |
| **Audience Orientation** | The memo assumes the reader knows the background (“Since Period 5…”) but does not explicitly state *why* senior management should care (impact on decision‑making, reporting timeliness). | Minto requires the **Situation** to be framed from the audience’s perspective, establishing relevance before presenting the complication. | Add a brief **relevance statement** in the Situation (e.g., “Accurate, timely graph books are critical for senior‑management decisions on resource allocation.”). |

---

### Summary of Required Changes

1. **Insert a clear top‑level answer** (key message) at the very start.  
2. **Apply SCQA**: Situation → Complication → Question → Answer.  
3. **Re‑structure supporting arguments** into MECE, hierarchical sections that roll up to the answer.  
4. **Replace vague discussion request with a concrete recommendation** and next steps.  
5. **Rewrite each bullet/paragraph as a single‑idea, concise statement**; eliminate redundancy.  
6. **Rename headings** to reflect the logical supporting themes.  
7. **Ensure formatting consistency** to visually convey hierarchy.  
8. **Add a relevance statement** to orient senior management to the importance of the issue.  

Implementing these changes will align the memo with the Minto Pyramid Principle, making it easier for senior management to grasp the problem, understand the analysis, and act on the proposed solution.
