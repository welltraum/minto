# Minto Audit: "Period Graph Books"

## Verdict

The memo has no governing thought. It is a chronological narrative of how work is done and what has gone wrong, ending in a request to talk. The reader reaches the last line without knowing what is being recommended, what it costs, or what decision is wanted. Management asked for corrective proposals; the document supplies a description.

---

## Defect 1: The main point is missing, not merely buried

The nearest thing to an answer sits in the penultimate paragraph — "the production process badly needs streamlining" — and it is a statement of need, not an answer to the reader's question. The reader's question is *What should we do about the errors?* An answer must name the action, the actor, and the outcome. "Needs streamlining" names none of these.

Worse, the memo's true message is only implied: **the process should be consolidated under single-point control, and doing so requires resources the current team does not have.** That is a two-part recommendation with a resource request attached. It appears nowhere as a statement.

## Defect 2: The closing question abdicates the pyramid

"Could we discuss how best to proceed?" transfers the analytical work back to the reader. The writer holds the process knowledge; the reader does not. A recommendation memo must arrive at a proposal the reader can accept, modify, or reject. An open invitation to discuss forces the reader to construct the pyramid during the meeting.

## Defect 3: No introduction; the reader is dropped into history

The opening states a fact of organizational history ("Since Period 5, Financial Analysis has been responsible...") and then announces the memo's own contents. There is no Situation–Complication–Question structure:

- **Situation** — the four books are produced for senior management presentations and must be accurate — is never stated. The purpose of the books is absent entirely, so the reader cannot judge the severity of anything that follows.
- **Complication** — errors and inconsistencies have appeared since the transfer — is scattered across the "Issues" section rather than established up front.
- **Question** — how do we eliminate the errors? — is never posed, which is why the memo can end by asking for a discussion instead of answering.

"This memo describes issues that appeared after the transfer" is a table of contents, not an introduction. It tells the reader what the document does rather than why the document exists.

## Defect 4: The "Production" section is time-order narrative masquerading as support

The four numbered steps describe procedure. They support nothing. In a pyramid they would appear only where they prove a point — for example, as evidence that the same data is handled by four parties. As written, they precede the problem statement, so the reader absorbs process detail before knowing what it is meant to illustrate. The reader must hold all four steps in memory and retro-fit them to the issues described two sections later.

## Defect 5: Grouping is by document section, not by logic

The three headings — Production, Issues, Overall assessment — are categories of exposition, not levels of an argument. Nothing under "Issues" is grouped:

- *fragmentation* (another person inserted; three departments; triple entry)
- *specific error types* (inconsistent data, wrong variable-cost calculations, unexplained point changes, re-entry of prior periods)
- *one book's detailed workflow*

These sit at three different levels of abstraction in adjacent paragraphs. The single-book example is a supporting illustration presented as a peer of the general finding. The error list is a set of symptoms presented as a peer of the fragmentation that causes them.

## Defect 6: Cause and effect are inverted in presentation

The memo lists symptoms first and diagnoses the cause last ("Most problems arise from a cumbersome fragmented process"). The causal claim is the interesting content and belongs above the symptoms, which then serve as its proof. As arranged, the reader performs the synthesis and the writer confirms it afterward — the reverse of the intended direction.

## Defect 7: The resource constraint is smuggled in

"The current team cannot manage the required changes as well as ongoing production" is the most consequential sentence in the memo. It is an implicit request for headcount or for relief from production duties, placed second-to-last, unquantified, and unlabeled. If this is the ask, it must be the ask — stated, sized, and justified.

## Defect 8: Unsupported and unquantified assertions

Several claims carry no evidence and cannot be acted upon:

- "the same information may be entered into computer systems three times" — *may be* hedges the central inefficiency claim. Either it does or it does not.
- "errors can fall between stages" — no instance given.
- "responsibility is unclear" — no statement of who currently holds it or should.
- Frequency, cost, and consequence of the errors are absent throughout. Nothing tells the reader whether an incorrect graph reached senior management.

## Defect 9: Deduction and induction are conflated

The final paragraph strings together premises that resemble a deductive chain — fragmented process → no end-to-end control → unclear responsibility → errors escape — but the conclusion drawn is not the chain's consequence. It is a separate assertion about streamlining, followed by a further separate assertion about team capacity. Three ideas are joined by adjacency rather than by logic.

---

## What must change

1. **State the answer as the title or opening line** — a specific corrective action, not a diagnosis of need.
2. **Build a proper introduction** establishing why the books matter, that they now contain errors, and the resulting question.
3. **Restructure to two or three key lines** that answer the question directly. The natural candidates: what should change in the process, who should own it, and what the change requires.
4. **Demote the process description** to evidence beneath whichever key line it supports, or cut it to an appendix.
5. **Group the errors under their cause** rather than listing them as coordinate findings.
6. **Promote the resource constraint** into an explicit, sized request.
7. **Quantify the harm** — error frequency, rework hours, any errors that reached presentation.
8. **Replace the closing question** with a recommendation and, if a meeting is genuinely needed, a stated purpose and agenda for it.
9. **Ensure the key lines are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive** — as written, "fragmented process," "no end-to-end control," and "unclear responsibility" substantially overlap and would collapse into one another under scrutiny.
