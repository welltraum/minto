## Minto Pyramid audit

The document has useful evidence, but it is not yet structured as a persuasive pyramid.

- The governing thought is buried. The central recommendation appears late and tentatively: the process needs streamlining and the current team cannot both redesign it and maintain production. This should be the opening answer, not an “overall assessment” near the end.

- The introduction lacks a clear SCQA sequence. It gives the situation (Financial Analysis inherited four books) and complication (errors/inconsistencies), but does not formulate the management question precisely. The implied question is: “What should management authorize to restore reliable production and redesign the process?” The document must answer that question immediately.

- The proposed action is incomplete. “Streamlining” is a direction, not a decision-ready recommendation. The document must state what management should approve—such as a named end-to-end owner, a single controlled data flow/calculation source, and dedicated redesign capacity—at the appropriate level of specificity.

- The main supporting ideas are not grouped under the recommendation. “Production,” “Issues,” and “Overall assessment” mix process description, symptoms, examples, root causes, and resource implications. They should be reorganized into a small set of mutually exclusive reasons supporting the recommendation.

- The reasoning chain is weak. The memo lists errors and duplicate entries, but does not clearly show: fragmented ownership and repeated transcription → inconsistent/uncontrolled data and calculations → unreliable graph books and excessive rework → need for process redesign and dedicated capacity.

- Evidence is presented before the point it proves. Detailed process steps and the single-book example should sit beneath the relevant supporting claim, not lead the document.

- The problem list is unprioritized and partly overlapping. Inconsistent data, incorrect calculations, changed points, re-entered history, multiple systems, and unclear responsibility are mixed together. Separate root causes from consequences, then group examples under each cause.

- The scope is unclear. The document alternates between one book and all four without explaining whether the identified failures are representative. It must distinguish the illustrative case from enterprise-wide findings.

- The close is too tentative. “Could we discuss how best to proceed?” retreats from the analysis. End with the decision or authorization required from management.
