# Audit: Period Graph Books Memo

## Governing Thought
Missing. The memo opens with background ("Since Period 5...") instead of a single top-level message. A reader finishing the first paragraph cannot state what the memo is arguing. The final line ("Could we discuss how best to proceed?") reveals that the real point — a recommendation or request for a decision — has been withheld until the end, and even then it's vague rather than a clear ask.

**Required governing thought (implied by content):** Something like: "The graph-book production process is broken because it is fragmented across too many hands with no single owner, and it should be redesigned and given to a dedicated team rather than patched by the current staff." The document needs to lead with this, not bury it.

## Structural Defects

**1. No pyramid — the document is chronological/narrative, not argument-driven.**
Sections are labeled "Production," "Issues," "Overall assessment" — a sequence of what-happened-when, not a hierarchy of a claim supported by grouped, mutually exclusive reasons. Minto's structure requires the top box (governing thought) to be decomposed into 2–4 supporting points that are themselves summarized by — not just a list leading up to — the top point.

**2. "Issues" section conflates two different logical levels.**
It mixes a general diagnosis (fragmentation, multiple entry points, no end-to-end owner) with a single detailed example (one specific book's process problems: inconsistent data, wrong variable-cost calculations, unexplained changes, re-entry). These are different levels of abstraction and should not sit in the same paragraph. The specific example is evidence for the general claim, not a co-equal point — currently it reads as an afterthought/aside rather than a supporting pillar.

**3. "Overall assessment" repeats the diagnosis instead of driving to action.**
This section restates the fragmentation problem a second time ("no one controls the data... responsibility is unclear... needs streamlining") without new grouping logic, then tacks on an unsupported claim ("the current team cannot manage the required changes") with no evidence given for it. This claim is important — it implies the recommendation (need a separate/dedicated team) — but it is asserted, not substantiated, and appears here almost incidentally rather than as a deliberate supporting leg of the argument.

**4. The four numbered "Production" steps are description, not argument.**
As written they support no claim — they're just process narration. In a Minto structure, these steps should only appear insofar as they supply evidence for *why* the process is fragmented (e.g., "three separate parties touch the same data," "manual transcription happens twice") — i.e., they belong under a supporting point, reordered and filtered to serve the argument, not reproduced as a standalone how-it-works section.

**5. Grouping is not MECE.**
"Issues" and "Overall assessment" overlap heavily (both discuss fragmentation and lack of ownership), meaning the reader has to do the work of merging them. A proper pyramid would consolidate these into one clean supporting line (e.g., "the process is fragmented" ) and keep a second, distinct line for the resourcing point ("the current team is stretched too thin to fix it while also running it") — currently these two ideas are tangled together in one closing paragraph instead of separated as parallel, non-overlapping supports.

**6. The action request is weak and unspecific.**
"Could we discuss how best to proceed?" is not a governing-thought-level call to action. It doesn't tell the reader what decision is being sought (approve a redesign? approve additional staff? approve a specific proposal?). Under Minto, the top of the pyramid should state the recommended action or decision needed, not defer it to a future undefined conversation.

## Summary of Required Changes
- Replace the opening narrative with a single governing thought stating the core message and the action wanted.
- Reorganize supports into 2–3 MECE groups (e.g., "process is fragmented across too many parties," "fragmentation causes recurring errors [with the one-book example as illustration]," "current team lacks capacity to redesign while sustaining production").
- Demote the four production steps to supporting evidence, trimmed to only what's needed to prove fragmentation — not a standalone procedural section.
- Merge "Issues" and "Overall assessment" to remove redundancy.
- Convert the closing question into a specific, actionable recommendation or decision request.
