# Minto Audit — "Period Graph Books"

## Verdict

The memo has no governing thought. It is organized by the *stages of the writer's inquiry* (here is the process, here is what went wrong, here is my overall impression) rather than by the *structure of the reader's question*. Senior management asked for corrective proposals; the memo delivers a description of symptoms and ends by asking for a meeting. The single most important defect: **the answer is missing.**

## Defects

**1. The introduction does not establish a Question.**

The opening paragraph gives Situation only ("Since Period 5, Financial Analysis has been responsible…") and then announces the memo's *topic* ("This memo describes issues") instead of the Complication that provokes a question. The reader's actual Situation–Complication–Question is available and unused:

- **S:** Financial Analysis took over production of four graph books in Period 5.
- **C:** Errors and inconsistencies have appeared since the transfer.
- **Q:** What should we do about it?

Because the Question is never posed, nothing in the document can be judged relevant or complete.

**2. The main point is buried and stated as a complaint, not a recommendation.**

The load-bearing sentence — "The production process badly needs streamlining" — sits in the penultimate paragraph, and even there it is a diagnosis rather than a course of action. The document is bottom-heavy: the reader must read all of it to learn its purpose. Under the Pyramid, the answer belongs first, and it must be an *actionable* answer — who does what, over what period, at what cost, with what expected effect. "Needs streamlining" commits no one to anything.

**3. "Could we discuss how best to proceed?" abdicates the writer's job.**

The closing question transfers the analytic work back to the reader. The response to "propose corrections" is a proposal, not an invitation to meet. This also leaves the final substantive claim — that the current team cannot absorb the change work alongside production — dangling as an aside, when it is in fact a resource request and should be argued as one.

**4. The "Production" section is a numbered list, not a supporting argument.**

The four steps stand at the top of the document as if they were key line points, but they support nothing. They answer a question no one asked ("how are the books made?"). Background of this kind belongs in the introduction, compressed to the minimum needed to make the argument intelligible — or in an appendix. As written, the reader's first encounter with the document is four steps of procedure whose purpose is unexplained.

**5. The key line is a grouping, not a reasoned argument.**

"Issues" collects observations of two different logical kinds, undifferentiated:

- *Structural facts:* another person inserted into a fragmented process; divisional staff plus three corporate departments; same data entered three times.
- *Consequences:* inconsistent data between periods and regions, incorrect variable-cost calculations, unexplained changes to correct points, prior-period data re-entered.

These belong on different levels of the pyramid: the structural facts are the **cause**, the consequences are the **effect**. Presenting them as one flat list severs the causal link that justifies the recommendation. The causal claim is asserted only later ("Most problems arise from a cumbersome fragmented process") and never demonstrated by the arrangement of the ideas themselves.

**6. Illustrative detail is mistaken for evidence.**

"For one book, divisional data are loaded into…" — a single unnamed book carries the whole weight of the argument about all four. The reader cannot tell whether this is the worst case, the typical case, or an isolated one, and therefore cannot tell whether the problem is general. Either the pattern across all four books is shown, or the example is explicitly labeled as representative and the basis for that label given.

**7. Deduction and induction are mixed within a single section.**

"Overall assessment" runs a deductive chain (no end-to-end ownership → unclear responsibility → errors fall between stages) inside what is otherwise an inductive list of complaints. Each group of supporting ideas must be one or the other, not both. Mixing them is why the section reads as accumulating grievance rather than as proof.

**8. Sections are not summarized; headings do no work.**

"Production," "Issues," "Overall assessment" name containers, not ideas. A reader skimming the headings learns nothing. Each heading should state the point the section proves, so the headings alone convey the argument.

## What Must Change

1. **Lead with the recommendation.** Open with the specific action proposed — consolidate end-to-end ownership of data and production for the four books in one place, eliminating the duplicate entry — stated as a decision the reader is being asked to approve.
2. **Restructure the key line as the reasoning for that action.** Three levels, in this order: the fragmented process causes the errors; consolidation removes the cause; consolidation requires resources the current team does not have.
3. **Demote the process description.** Move the four steps into the introduction (or an appendix) and use them only to make the triple-entry problem visible.
4. **Subordinate the symptoms to their cause.** The specific errors become evidence *under* the causal claim, not a peer of it.
5. **Convert the closing question into a request for decision.** State what is needed — staffing, timing, authority — and what happens if the process is left as is.
6. **Rewrite every heading as a full-sentence point,** so the headings read as a summary of the argument.
7. **Establish the scope of the evidence.** Show the pattern across all four books, or state plainly why one book stands for all of them.
