# Audit Result: Period Graph Books

**Pyramid status:** Non-compliant. The memo is bottom-up, descriptive, and not sufficiently action-oriented.

## Core diagnosis

The document explains what happens in the production process and lists problems, but it does not lead with the main conclusion or the decision needed. The central point—that the process is fragmented, poorly controlled, and needs streamlining—appears only near the end. Because management asked for corrective proposals, the memo should be organized around a clear recommendation or decision request, not around a description of issues.

## Defects

### 1. No answer-first governing thought

- The memo opens with background: “Since Period 5…” and “This memo describes issues…”
- The main conclusion is delayed until “Overall assessment.”
- The reader must read through process detail and problem descriptions before learning the essential point.
- In Minto terms, the top of the pyramid is missing.

### 2. Weak subject line

- “Subject: Period Graph Books” is only a topic label.
- It does not tell the reader what is being recommended, decided, or requested.
- The subject line should function as a key line that communicates the main message or decision sought.

### 3. Ineffective opening / missing SCQA logic

- The opening gives some situation but does not frame the complication, question, and answer in a way that leads to a conclusion.
- It does not clearly connect:
  - the transfer of responsibility,
  - the resulting errors and inconsistencies,
  - the need for corrective action,
  - the decision or proposal being requested.
- The memo therefore feels like a status report rather than a persuasive or decision-oriented communication.

### 4. Structure is not top-down

- The sections are arranged topically: “Production,” “Issues,” “Overall assessment.”
- The production steps appear before the point they are meant to support.
- The headings are labels, not key-line statements.
- The document does not build a hierarchy in which each lower level answers the question raised by the level above it.

### 5. Broken vertical logic

- After the opening sentence, the reader naturally asks: “What issues appeared?”
- The next section, “Production,” does not answer that question; it describes the process.
- The reader then has to wait until “Issues” and finally “Overall assessment” to understand why the process matters.
- The vertical question-and-answer relationship is weak.

### 6. Mixed levels and poor grouping

- The “Issues” section combines several different types of material in one block:
  - a cause: the transfer added another person to a fragmented process;
  - scope: divisional staff and three corporate departments participate;
  - mechanism: information may be entered three times;
  - a specific example: one book’s data path;
  - symptoms: inconsistent data, incorrect calculations, unexplained changes, re-entry of prior-period data.
- These ideas are not parallel.
- They are not grouped by a clear logical category.
- They are not mutually exclusive or collectively exhaustive.

### 7. Causes and symptoms are blurred

- The real structural problem is fragmentation, lack of end-to-end ownership, and repeated handoffs.
- The memo buries that insight in the final section.
- The listed “problems” are mostly symptoms or consequences, not organized root causes.
- As a result, the reader cannot easily see which issues share the same underlying cause.

### 8. The main conclusion is compressed and underdeveloped

- “Overall assessment” contains several important ideas:
  - the process is fragmented;
  - no one controls the data from beginning to end;
  - responsibility is unclear;
  - errors can fall between stages;
  - the process needs streamlining for individual books and common calculations;
  - the current team cannot manage the changes while continuing production.
- These are potentially strong key lines, but they are packed into one paragraph.
- They are not developed as separate supporting arguments.

### 9. Not action-oriented

- Management asked for corrective proposals.
- The memo provides no clear proposal, option, recommendation, or decision request.
- It ends with “Could we discuss how best to proceed?”, which is vague and passive.
- A Minto-style action memo should state what action is wanted, what decision is needed, and what the next step should be.

### 10. Audience and detail mismatch

- The numbered “Production” steps are operational detail.
- They are introduced too early and are more granular than necessary for a senior-management decision memo.
- They interrupt the main message instead of supporting a specific key line.
- Such detail is better placed in an appendix or used briefly as evidence under a point such as “the current process involves multiple handoffs and duplicate entries.”

## What must change

### 1. Put the governing thought first

- The memo must open with the main conclusion or decision needed.
- The reader should immediately understand that the process needs to be redesigned, streamlined, or given clearer ownership.
- The current “Overall assessment” should effectively become the opening message.

### 2. Make the subject line a key line

- The subject line should state the main point or decision sought, not merely name the topic.
- It should tell the reader what the memo is about in terms of action or conclusion.

### 3. Use a proper SCQA lead-in

- The opening should frame:
  - **Situation:** Financial Analysis now produces the four monthly graph books.
  - **Complication:** Errors and inconsistencies have appeared since the transfer.
  - **Question:** What should be done to correct the process?
  - **Answer:** The governing thought / recommendation / decision needed.
- This will turn the memo from a description into a decision-oriented communication.

### 4. Rebuild the document as a true pyramid

- The top should be the governing thought.
- Below it should be a small number of key lines that support that thought.
- Each key line should then be supported by grouped evidence, examples, or analysis.
- The current sections should be reorganized so that every part supports the main message.

### 5. Convert headings into key-line statements

- Headings such as “Production,” “Issues,” and “Overall assessment” are too generic.
- Each heading should express a complete point that supports the governing thought.
- The reader should be able to understand the argument from the headings alone.

### 6. Separate causes from symptoms

- The issues should be organized by underlying cause or process weakness, not simply by observed error.
- Possible logical groupings would include:
  - multiple handoffs and fragmented ownership;
  - duplicate data entry;
  - lack of a single end-to-end data controller;
  - manual or inconsistent calculation methods;
  - inadequate checking or validation;
  - capacity constraints on the current team.
- The specific errors should then be presented as evidence under the relevant cause.

### 7. Make the grouping MECE

- Ideas at the same level should be:
  - the same type of idea;
  - mutually exclusive;
  - collectively sufficient to support the point.
- The current “Issues” section mixes causes, examples, scope, mechanisms, and symptoms, so it must be regrouped.

### 8. Establish a clear logical order

- The supporting points should be arranged in a deliberate order, such as:
  - importance,
  - urgency,
  - process sequence,
  - cause-to-effect,
  - or problem-to-solution.
- The current order feels descriptive rather than argumentative.

### 9. Demote operational detail

- The production steps should not dominate the main body.
- They should be moved to an appendix or reduced to brief evidence supporting a specific point about fragmentation, duplication, or lack of control.
- Senior management needs the implication of the process, not necessarily every operational step.

### 10. Make the request explicit

- The closing should not merely invite discussion.
- It should state the action being requested, such as:
  - approval to review or redesign the process;
  - agreement on a pilot scope;
  - assignment of a process owner;
  - definition of next steps;
  - scheduling of a decision meeting.
- The memo should align with management’s request for corrective proposals.

### 11. Strengthen the action orientation

- The document should move from “here are the problems” to “here is what should be done and what decision is needed.”
- The tone should be direct and decisive rather than exploratory.
- The final request should be specific enough that the reader knows what is expected of them.
