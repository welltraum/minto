# Fixture 02: TTW composing costs

**Mode:** `write`
**Language:** `en`

## Context

TTW is a printing company in Aylesbury. A junior consultant spent two weeks
examining composing-room costs. The engagement manager already knows that
composition accounts for roughly 40% of hardback cost and 50-55% of paperback
cost, that TTW is uncompetitive on simple work, and that TTW does not know
whether its composing costs are actually excessive.

## Before

```text
To:
From:
Subject: TTW

This note records the information gathered during the past two weeks.

Composition is the largest production cost. Industry comparisons suggest weak
productivity and high cost at TTW. Every title, whether a complex reference
book or a simple novel, passes through essentially the same quality-control
stages.

People in Aylesbury want to understand the causes. I spoke with Roy Walter,
Brian Thompson, and George Kennedy. Kennedy is willing to test whether some
stages can be removed and why measured productivity trails the benchmark.

The department is overloaded. Manual composition is especially short of
capacity. TTW pays below local market rates, struggles to hire and retain
compositors, has just lost two employees, and now runs most jobs late.
Overtime is more than 50% over budget, while the union is pressing a new claim.

Conclusions

1. Costs may be reduced by:
   a. simplifying the process for inexpensive titles;
   b. increasing productivity by changing the composing method.

2. To simplify the process, TTW should test selected jobs and measure the
   effect of fewer or differently timed checks on quality and customers. The
   saving might reach 10% of total composing cost. A separate methods study
   could identify further productivity improvements because TTW trails the
   benchmark by 20-50%.

3. Comparisons with three other printers might provide useful context, although
   I doubt a simple comparison will settle the issue.

4. Managers disagree about whether costs are high, but all are willing to
   investigate.
```

## Task

Rewrite the report using the Minto Pyramid Principle. Keep the useful facts,
remove or demote material that does not support the recommendation, and state
what was omitted.
